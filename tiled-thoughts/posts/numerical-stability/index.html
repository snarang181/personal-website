Why Numerical Stability is a Compiler Concern

Exploring the critical role of numerical stability in compiler design, particularly in the context of MLIR and GPU programming for deep learning applications.
November 13, 2025 · 1 min · Samarth Narang
Table of Contents

When people discuss numerical stability, the focus often falls on the algorithm itself:

“Use the numerically stable softmax.”

“This normalization is unstable in fp16.”

But if you work on compilers or kernel code generation, you’ve likely encountered the other side of the coin:

“It was stable in the Python reference… until we ‘optimized’ it.”s

In this post, I want to highlight a crucial point:

Numerical stability is not just a mathematical problem — it’s a compiler problem.

Defining Numerical Stability#

Informally:

A numerically stable algorithm ensures that small floating-point errors do not escalate into significant inaccuracies.

Every operation in floating point arithmetic is inherently approximate. The key question is: do small rounding errors remain small, or do they amplify into NaNs, infs, or completely erroneous gradients as computations progress?

A few classic indicators of instability include:

  • Encountering NaN or inf for “reasonable” inputs.
  • Minor changes in input (or operation ordering) leading to substantial output variations.
  • Discrepancies between results computed in higher precision (e.g., float64) versus float16/float32.

Let’s ground this discussion in the usual suspect: softmax.

Softmax: A Classic Example of Numerical Stability#

$$ \mathrm{softmax}(x_i) = \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}}. $$
